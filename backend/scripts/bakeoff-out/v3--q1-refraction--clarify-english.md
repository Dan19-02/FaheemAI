Light slows down the moment it enters water, and that change of speed is what bends it.

Here is the picture: a pencil is sitting half in water, half in air. Light bounces off every part of the pencil and travels to your eye. The light from the underwater part has to cross the water surface. At that surface, the light slows down (it travels slower in water than in air), and when light changes speed at a boundary, it bends. The light from the part still in air does not bend at all.

So your eye receives:

- straight-line rays from the dry part of the pencil
- bent rays from the wet part of the pencil

Your brain always assumes light came in a straight line, so it traces those bent rays backward in a straight line. That makes the underwater tip appear shifted, and the whole pencil looks broken or bent at the surface.

The rule of thumb: light bends toward the normal when it enters a denser medium (air to water), and away from the normal when it leaves (water to air). That bending is exactly why the apparent depth of a coin at the bottom of water is less than its real depth.

A small trap students fall into: the pencil is not actually bent, and the light ray is not actually broken. The bending happens only at the water surface, and your brain misinterprets where the underwater tip is.

Quick check: if you looked at the pencil perfectly straight down (along the normal), would it still look bent? Why or why not?
