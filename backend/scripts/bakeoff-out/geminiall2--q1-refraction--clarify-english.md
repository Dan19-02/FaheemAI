Light actually changes its speed when it travels from water into air, and this sudden change in speed forces the light rays to bend. Think of a shopping cart rolling from a tiled floor onto a thick carpet at an angle. The wheels that hit the carpet first slow down, causing the cart to pivot and change direction. 

When you look at the submerged pencil, light reflecting from it speeds up as it exits the water into the air, bending away from the normal line. Your brain assumes light only travels in straight lines, so it projects these bent rays straight back. This makes the underwater portion appear shifted upward, creating the illusion of a bend at the surface.

While you met this concept in Class 10, it returns with deeper mathematical derivations in Class 12 Ray Optics [CBSE]. A common trap in exam diagrams is drawing the light bending the wrong way: remember that light bends *toward* the normal when entering a denser medium, but *away* from the normal when escaping into a rarer medium like air [Refraction of Light · Class 10].

If you look at a coin sitting at the bottom of a bucket of water, based on how the light rays bend as they exit the water, do you think the coin will appear deeper than it actually is, or shallower?
