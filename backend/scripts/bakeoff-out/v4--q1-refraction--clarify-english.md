That bending you saw is refraction in action, and it happens because **light slows down when it enters water**.

Here is the key idea: light travels at different speeds in different materials. In air it is fastest, in water it is slower, in glass even slower. When a light ray hits the water surface at an angle, the part that enters first slows down while the rest is still zipping through air. That little tug bends the ray.

Now think about the pencil. Light bounces off every part of the pencil (the part above water AND the part below water) and then travels to your eyes. The rays from the underwater part bend at the surface, so your brain, which assumes light always travels in straight lines, traces those rays back in a straight line and places the underwater tip somewhere it actually is not. The pencil looks "broken" or bent at the water line, even though the real pencil is perfectly straight.

A simple way to picture it: imagine a toy car rolling diagonally from a smooth floor onto a rough carpet at an angle. The wheel that hits the carpet first slows down, so the car swerves. Light does the same thing at the water surface.

One real trap students fall into: thinking the water itself bends the pencil. It does not. The pencil is straight. Your eyes are being fooled by bent light, so the image your brain builds is bent.

Quick check: if you looked at the pencil from directly above (straight down, not at an angle), would it still look bent? Think about why or why not, and tell me your reasoning.
