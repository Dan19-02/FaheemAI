That bent-pencil thing is one of the most beautiful everyday tricks light plays on us, and the reason is hiding in one word: **speed**.

Light travels at different speeds in different materials. In air it zips along at full speed, but the moment it enters water, it has to slow down. And when light changes speed, it bends. That's refraction in one line.

Now picture the pencil half-dipped in water. The light bouncing off the pencil travels up to your eyes, but the rays coming from the *underwater* part have to cross the water-air boundary. They slow down (or speed up) at that boundary, and that change in speed is what bends their path.

Here's the part that trips most students: your brain doesn't know the light bent. It assumes light always travels in straight lines, so it traces the ray *backward in a straight line* from your eye. That straight-line trace lands at a different spot than the real pencil tip, so your brain "sees" the pencil tip in the wrong place. The underwater part looks lifted up, which is why the whole pencil appears broken or bent at the water's surface.

The rule to remember:
- Light going from **air into water** (fast to slow) bends **toward** the normal.
- Light going from **water into air** (slow to fast) bends **away** from the normal.

A small but real trap students fall into: they think the pencil is *actually* bending. It isn't. The pencil is perfectly straight. The image your brain constructs is the bent one, because your brain insists on straight-line vision.

This is from your Class 10 Light chapter (Reflection and Refraction), and you'll meet it again in Class 12 Ray Optics, so getting this clear now will pay off twice.
